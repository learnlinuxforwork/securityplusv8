Lab guide 8

Data Protection, Resilience, and Recovery

Protecting data at rest, in transit, and in use — then making sure the whole environment survives a bad day.

10-12 hours Week 7 complete
Before you start
  • secure-target VM up

1. Data states and classification

Data exists in three states, each needing a different protection: at rest (encrypted disk/database), in transit (TLS), and in use (harder — protections here include secure enclaves and careful memory handling). CompTIA's classification scale runs roughly: public, confidential, sensitive, critical, restricted, secret, top secret — the exact labels an organization uses vary, but the principle is the same: not all data deserves the same protection budget.

2. Mask, hash, and tokenize a sample dataset

Three different ways to protect data that look similar but solve different problems.

Masking — hide part of the value, reversible by design if you keep the mapping
echo '4111-1111-1111-1234' | sed -E 's/[0-9]{4}-[0-9]{4}-[0-9]{4}/****-****-****/'
Hashing — one-way, good for verifying integrity or storing passwords
echo -n 'sensitive-record-42' | sha256sum
Tokenization — replace with a reference value that means nothing outside your own vault
echo 'Original: account 4111111111111234 -> Token: TOK_9f8e7d6c5b4a'

3. Data protection roles

Data owner: accountable for the data's classification and protection, usually a business role. Data custodian: implements the technical controls the owner requires. Data steward: manages data quality and usage policy day-to-day. Data controller (a legal/privacy term): decides why and how data is processed. Data processor/subprocessor: processes data on the controller's behalf, under contract. In a data breach, these are exactly the roles regulators ask you to name.

4. Data handling: geofencing, marking, and the data lifecycle

Marking and labeling makes classification visible (a document header, a metadata tag). Geofencing restricts where data can be accessed from or where it's allowed to reside — directly relevant to data sovereignty requirements. The full data management life cycle — creation, management, distribution, retention, and disposal — means "secure" isn't a one-time state; data needs protection differently at each stage, including secure disposal at the end.

5. Calculate recovery metrics from a scenario

Four acronyms that get tested constantly, worked from real numbers.

Given: an outage started at 09:00, was detected at 09:15, restored at 11:00, last good backup was 08:00
# RTO (target max downtime) is a PLANNING number set in advance, e.g. RTO = 2 hours -> this outage (2h) just met it
# RPO (max acceptable data loss) is also planned, e.g. RPO = 1 hour -> actual data loss here was 1 hour (08:00 backup to 09:00 outage) -> met it
# MTTR here = 11:00 - 09:15 (detection to restore) = 1h45m
# MTBF needs multiple incidents over time to calculate — it's an average, not a single-incident number

6. Site considerations and redundancy

A hot site is fully operational and ready to fail over to immediately — expensive, fastest recovery. A warm site has infrastructure but needs some setup. A cold site has space and power only — cheapest, slowest recovery. Redundancy strategies — load balancing, clustering, autoscaling, high availability configurations, and layered power protection (UPS for short outages, a generator for long ones, a surge protector against spikes) — exist to avoid needing the recovery site at all.
