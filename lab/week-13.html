Lab guide 13

Governance, Risk, and Third-Party Risk

The first GRC week — the paperwork that decides what "secure enough" means for a given organization.

8-10 hours Week 12 complete
Before you start
  • No lab VM needed this week — this is a reading and worksheet week, and that's intentional, not a shortcut

1. Guideline, standard, procedure, policy — four different documents

A guideline is a recommendation ("consider doing X"). A standard is a mandatory baseline ("passwords must be 12+ characters"). A procedure is the exact steps to do something (a runbook). A policy is the organization's stated position and requirement ("all remote access requires MFA"). Mixing these up in conversation is common; mixing them up on the exam costs you points on scenario questions that hinge on exactly this distinction.

2. Read a real AUP and BYOD policy

Find a publicly posted Acceptable Use Policy or BYOD policy from any organization (many universities and companies publish these). Identify three concrete, enforceable requirements in it — not vague statements, but things you could actually check compliance against (e.g. "personal devices must have a PIN or biometric lock enabled" is checkable; "be responsible with company data" is not).

3. Build a risk register entry

The actual artifact a security team maintains, not just a concept.

A minimal risk register row for a real risk on your own lab
cat > ~/secplus-lab/risk-register.txt <<'EOF'
Asset: secure-target SSH access
Risk: Brute-force credential attack against exposed SSH
Likelihood: Medium
Impact: High
Owner: [your name]
Current mitigation: firewalld rate-limiting, key-based auth only
Residual risk: Low
EOF
cat ~/secplus-lab/risk-register.txt

4. Calculate ALE from a scenario

Single loss expectancy times annualized rate of occurrence, worked from real numbers.

Given: a ransomware incident costs an estimated $50,000 (SLE) and historically happens about once every 4 years (ARO = 0.25)
python3 -c "SLE=50000; ARO=0.25; print('ALE =', SLE*ARO)"
# ALE = $12,500/year — this is the number that justifies (or doesn't) a given year's security spend against this specific risk

5. Risk treatment: four options, one scenario each

Transferring risk: buying cyber insurance. Accepting risk: the cost of the control exceeds the cost of the risk, so you document the decision and move on. Avoiding risk: discontinuing the risky activity entirely (e.g. not collecting data you don't need). Mitigating risk: implementing a control that reduces likelihood or impact. Every real risk decision is one of these four — including "we'll deal with it later," which is actually acceptance, whether anyone admits it or not.

6. Vendor agreements, matched to purpose

RFP/RFI/RFQ: the vendor selection process, in that rough order (information, then proposal, then quote). SLA: defines the service level a vendor commits to (uptime, response time). MOU/MOA: a non-binding or lightly-binding statement of intent between parties. NDA: protects confidential information shared during the relationship. MSA: the master contract terms that govern the whole relationship. SOW: defines the specific work for a specific engagement under that MSA. Getting these right on the exam usually means matching the RIGHT document to a one-sentence scenario.

Check your work

Run these before you consider the lab finished.

  1. risk-register.txt exists with all six fields filled in for a real risk
  2. Your ALE calculation matches SLE × ARO exactly
  3. You can name the correct document type (SLA/MOU/NDA/MSA/SOW) for five different one-sentence vendor scenarios
  4. You identified three concrete, checkable requirements in a real AUP or BYOD policy
