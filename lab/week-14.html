Lab guide 14

Compliance, Audits, and Security Awareness

The last content week — proving you're compliant, testing that you actually are, and teaching everyone else.

8-10 hours Week 13 complete
Before you start
  • No lab VM needed this week — reading, scenario practice, and one design exercise

1. Consequences of non-compliance

Non-compliance isn't abstract: reputational damage (customer trust, press coverage), financial penalties (regulatory fines, which can be enormous under frameworks like GDPR), legal exposure (lawsuits), contractual breach (losing a customer contract that required certain controls), sanctions, and in extreme cases loss of license to operate in a given industry at all. Each has a different owner in the organization who cares most about it — which is exactly why compliance work touches every department.

2. Privacy concepts: controller, processor, and individual rights

A data controller decides why and how personal data is processed; a data processor processes it on the controller's behalf, under contract. Right to be forgotten lets an individual request deletion of their data. Opt-in vs. opt-out describes the default consent posture (opt-in is more protective of the individual). Data correction and processing restriction/prevention are additional rights an individual can exercise — these show up constantly in scenario questions phrased as "a customer requests X, what regulation/right applies?"

3. Distinguish known, unknown, and partially known pen tests

A scoping decision that changes everything about how a test runs.

Write out the three scopes in your own words, with a scenario for each
cat > ~/secplus-lab/pentest-scopes.txt <<'EOF'
Known environment: tester has full documentation/access up front - tests defenses assuming an insider or well-documented attacker
Unknown environment: tester starts with zero information - simulates a real external attacker from scratch
Partially known: tester has some info (e.g. IP ranges) but not full access - the realistic middle ground most real engagements use
EOF
cat ~/secplus-lab/pentest-scopes.txt

4. MITRE ATT&CK and the Cyber Kill Chain — two different models

The Cyber Kill Chain describes an attack as a linear sequence of stages (reconnaissance through actions on objectives) — useful for thinking about where in a sequence to interrupt an attack. MITRE ATT&CK is a much more granular, non-linear matrix of specific real-world tactics and techniques, used heavily for threat intelligence and detection engineering. The Diamond Model frames an intrusion as four connected points: adversary, capability, infrastructure, and victim. All three are reference models for the same underlying problem — knowing when each is the right tool matters more than memorizing their stage names.

5. Design a security awareness training outline

The deliverable this domain has been building toward — you're the one designing the training now, not just taking it.

A one-page outline for a sample organization
cat > ~/secplus-lab/awareness-outline.txt <<'EOF'
Initial/onboarding: phishing recognition, password policy, acceptable use - day 1
Ongoing: quarterly phishing simulation + 15-min refresher
Targeted: extra fraud-prevention training for Finance/AP staff specifically
Corrective: mandatory 1:1 retraining for anyone who clicks a simulated phish
Delivery: LMS for initial/ongoing, one-to-one for corrective
Metrics: click-rate on simulations, completion rate, time-to-report a real phish
EOF
cat ~/secplus-lab/awareness-outline.txt
