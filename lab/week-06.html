Lab guide 6

Indicators of Malicious Activity

The biggest single objective in the exam. Malware families, network attacks, social engineering, and how to read an indicator of compromise.

10-12 hours Week 5 complete
Before you start
  • secure-target and kali-tools on the SAME isolated, host-only network — no bridge to your real network or the internet
  • This week's traffic-capture and lockout steps happen only against your own VMs

1. Ten malware categories, by behavior not just name

Ransomware encrypts data for extortion. A Trojan disguises itself as legitimate software. A worm self-propagates without user action; a virus needs a host file and usually user action. Spyware and a keylogger both exfiltrate information quietly. Adware is the (usually) least dangerous but still unwanted. A rootkit hides itself at a privileged level of the OS. A logic bomb sits dormant until a trigger condition. Fileless malware lives in memory or abuses legitimate system tools, leaving little on disk to find.

2. Capture and read real traffic on your isolated lab network

The single most useful diagnostic skill in this entire domain.

From kali-tools, capture a short window of traffic to/from secure-target
sudo tcpdump -i eth0 host secure-target.lab -c 20 -nn
# Generate some traffic from another terminal on secure-target first: ping -c 5 kali-tools.lab

3. Physical attacks: the low-tech ones that still work

Tailgating (following someone through a badge-locked door), shoulder surfing (watching someone type a password), skimming (a card-reader overlay), and forced entry are all physical-layer attacks with no exploit code involved at all — and they remain effective precisely because organizations over-invest in technical controls and under-invest in physical awareness.

4. Network attacks: DDoS, spoofing, on-path, and DNS

A DDoS overwhelms availability from many sources at once. Spoofing forges a source address or identity. An on-path attack (formerly "man-in-the-middle") intercepts traffic between two parties who believe they're talking directly. DNS cache poisoning corrupts a resolver's cached records to redirect victims to a malicious destination without them noticing anything wrong in the URL bar.

5. Trigger and find an account lockout

One of the clearest indicators of compromise you'll ever generate on purpose.

Deliberately fail a login a few times, then find the evidence
ssh baduser@secure-target.lab   # will fail — that's the point, try it 3-4 times
sudo journalctl -u sshd --since '5 min ago' --no-pager 2>/dev/null | grep -i fail

6. Social engineering: know the specific names

Phishing (email), smishing (SMS), vishing (voice/phone), and quishing (malicious QR code) are the same con across different channels. Spear phishing targets a specific person with tailored context; whaling specifically targets executives. A deepfake takes impersonation into fabricated audio/video. All of them exploit trust and urgency, not a technical flaw.

7. Reading an indicator of compromise

A real IoC list from an incident might include: a file hash, a malicious IP address or domain, a malicious process name, file system artifacts (files dropped in unusual locations), a suspicious timestamp (activity at 3 AM local time), log manipulation (gaps or edited entries — often a bigger red flag than the original event), excessive resource consumption, plaintext strings found in a memory dump, an account lockout, impossible travel (same account, two countries, ten minutes apart), and concurrent sessions from different locations.

Check your work

Run these before you consider the lab finished.

  1. tcpdump output shows real packets captured between the two VMs
  2. journalctl output shows the failed login attempts you generated
  3. You can name all four social engineering channels (phishing/smishing/vishing/quishing) and the two escalated variants (spear phishing, whaling)
  4. You can list at least eight distinct types of indicators of compromise from memory
