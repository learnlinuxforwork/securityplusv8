Lab guide 12

Incident Response and Digital Forensics

The last Security Operations week — what happens after the alert fires, and how you prove what happened afterward.

10-12 hours Week 11 complete
Before you start
  • secure-target VM up
  • A quiet block of time for the tabletop exercise — it's the core of this week

1. The incident response lifecycle, in order

Preparation (training, playbooks, defined roles) happens before anything goes wrong. Identification (detection, internal/external advisories, threat hunting) is where you first realize something's wrong. Investigation (digital forensics, chain of custody, preservation) figures out what actually happened. Containment (quarantine/isolation) stops it from getting worse. Eradication/recovery removes the threat and restores normal operation. Notification/reporting — to stakeholders, customers, and sometimes law enforcement or regulators (mandatory in many jurisdictions for certain data types) — happens in parallel with recovery, not after. Post-incident review (lessons learned, root cause analysis, a formal post-incident report) closes the loop.

2. Run a tabletop exercise

Read this scenario, then talk through your response out loud, stage by stage, as if it were real: "At 2:14 AM, your monitoring alerts on unusual outbound traffic from secure-target to an unfamiliar IP address. journalctl shows three failed SSH logins for the 'webops' account at 2:09 AM, followed by a successful login at 2:11 AM from an IP address you don't recognize." Walk it: what do you check first (identification)? What do you isolate and how (containment)? What evidence do you preserve before you touch anything else (investigation)? Who do you notify, and when (notification)?

3. Preserve evidence properly

Chain of custody isn't paperwork for its own sake — it's what makes evidence usable later, including legally.

Document exactly what you're about to look at, before you look at it
date
whoami
sha256sum /var/log/secure 2>/dev/null || sha256sum /var/log/auth.log
# Recording the hash BEFORE you touch a log file proves it wasn't altered during your investigation

4. Capture a memory dump and a disk snapshot

Two different artifacts, two different jobs.

A lightweight process-memory example (a full RAM dump needs a dedicated tool like LiME in a real investigation)
cat /proc/[PID]/maps 2>/dev/null | head -5   # replace [PID] with a real process id from `ps aux`
A disk-level snapshot, conceptually (use your hypervisor's actual snapshot feature for the real thing)
# In VirtualBox/VMware: take a VM snapshot right now, before any remediation — that snapshot IS your bit-level forensic copy

5. Match data source to investigative question

Which source actually answers each question: "Who logged in and when?" → authentication logs. "What did the traffic look like?" → packet captures / NetFlow. "Was this file changed?" → file/log integrity checks (hashes taken before and after). "Who was physically in the building?" → surveillance footage. "What does the vulnerability landscape look like?" → vulnerability scans. Picking the wrong source wastes the most valuable resource in an active incident: time.

6. Who else gets pulled in

A real incident isn't just a technical response. HR gets involved for insider-threat cases. Legal gets involved for anything with regulatory notification requirements, litigation hold implications, or law enforcement contact. Accounts/Finance gets involved for anything with a financial-fraud angle. Knowing to loop these stakeholders in early — not after you've already made decisions that affect them — is itself a tested skill.

Check your work

Run these before you consider the lab finished.

  1. You completed the tabletop exercise through all seven IR lifecycle stages, out loud or in writing
  2. You can explain why hashing a log BEFORE investigating it matters for chain of custody
  3. You can match at least five investigative questions to the correct data source
  4. You took an actual VM snapshot as this week's disk-image exercise
