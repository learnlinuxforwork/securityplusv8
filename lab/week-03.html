Lab guide 3

Threat Characteristics and Threat Actors

Who's attacking, why, and how you'd actually know a threat is credible.

8-10 hours Week 2 complete
Before you start
  • secure-target and kali-tools VMs both up, on the same isolated host-only network

1. Threats vs. vulnerabilities — the difference that matters

A vulnerability is a weakness. A threat is something that could exploit it. An unpatched web server is a vulnerability whether or not anyone ever attacks it; a threat actor scanning the internet for that exact vulnerability is the threat. Risk is what happens when the two meet.

2. Look up a real CVE and read its CVSS score

The Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures database and the Common Vulnerability Scoring System are how the industry talks about severity precisely instead of vaguely.

Pull a specific CVE's details (requires internet access from your normal machine, not the isolated lab network)
# In a browser: https://nvd.nist.gov/vuln/detail/CVE-2021-44228
# Note the CVSS base score and vector string (AV/AC/PR/UI/S/C/I/A) — that string IS the score, decoded.

3. Threat feeds, likelihood, impact, and life cycle

A threat feed is a subscribed source of current, active threat data — IPs, domains, hashes seen in real attacks. Prioritizing what to act on combines likelihood (how probable is this against us specifically) with impact (how bad if it happens). A threat has a life cycle too: it's discovered, weaponized, deployed, and eventually becomes commodity/well-defended — timing your defenses against where a threat sits in that cycle matters.

4. Eight threat actor types, matched to motivation

Match each actor to its typical driver: organized crime (financial), state-sponsored (espionage, political), hacktivist (ideological, political), insider (varies — financial, revenge, or accidental/unintentional with no malicious motive at all), unskilled attacker (notoriety, curiosity — low sophistication but not zero risk), terrorist (fear/chaos, ideological), competitor (intellectual property, financial). Two attributes cut across all of them: internal vs. external, and level of sophistication/resources — a state-sponsored actor and an unskilled attacker can pursue the same target with wildly different capability.

5. See an unauthenticated scan from the attacker's side

A quick, safe demonstration of reconnaissance — the first thing almost any threat actor does.

From kali-tools, discover what secure-target exposes (your own isolated lab network only)
nmap -sV secure-target.lab   # or its lab IP address
# Read the output: every open port is a piece of your attack surface an actor now knows about

Check your work

Run these before you consider the lab finished.

  1. nmap output shows at least one open port on secure-target (likely ssh)
  2. You can name the motivation most associated with each of the eight threat actor types, without notes
  3. You can explain, in one sentence, what CVSS's base score represents
